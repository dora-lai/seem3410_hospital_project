1 What is simulation?
Consider a single-server queue . Customers
arrive over time, each requiring service that
lasts for a certain duration. Whenever the
server becomes free, she immediately begins
serving the next waiting customer.
Suppose you are a prophet : you know every customer’s arrival time and service
duration in advance.
Could you determine the queue length at any time? Yes.
• Record events in time order: arrivals, service starts, and completions.
• At each event, update system state : who is waiting and who is in service.
• Given the initial state and service rules, these prophet inputs determine one
sample path of the system evolution .
• However, such bookkeeping becomes tedious for large systems.
Computer simulation uses code to automate this bookkeeping. One
execution based on one set of inputs is called a simulation run .

Random inputs and repeated runs
That average comes from one set of inputs. In practice, arrival times and service
durations are uncertain, so we describe them using probability distributions .
1. For each simulation run, the prophet draws fresh inputs from these
distributions .
2. Apply the bookkeeping rules to produce one possible sample path of the
system trajectory. Calculate performance measures from this trajectory.
3. Repeat with independent input draws for each run, while keeping the model,
probability distributions, and system initial conditions the same.
Repeated runs let us assess average performance and variability .
4. For each run, a simulation program can generate inputs in advance or as it
needs them.

Discrete-event simulation
For the single-server example, the bookkeeping within each run consititues a
discrete-event simulation (DES) .
• DES represents the operation of a system as a discrete sequence of events in
time.
• Each event occurs at a particular time point and marks a change of state in
the system, such as a customer arrival, a service start, or completion.
• The state describes the system at a given time, such as the number of
customers waiting and whether the server is busy.
Between consecutive events, no change in the system occurs, so the simulation
clock can directly jump to the occurrence time of the next event .
• This feature makes DES more eﬀicient than incremental-time simulation ,
where time is broken up into small time slices and the system state is updated
according to the set of events happening in the time slice.
• This is because not every time slice has to be simulated .

2. Future-event list: what happens next?
The future-event list (FEL) records pending events that await execution. Each
entry stores an event time and an event type , e.g., (2 , A 3 ) means customer 3
arrives at t = 2 .
Its role in the simulation loop:
1. Select the earliest event time , advance the clock to that time, and remove
the entry from the FEL.
2. The event type determines the routine used to update the system state .
3. Add entries scheduled by the event routine; retain other pending entries and
repeat.

3. Event routine: model-specific simulation logic
An event routine specifies what to do when a particular type of event occurs. It
implements the logic of the simulation model .
• The simulator selects and removes the earliest FEL entry, advances the clock,
and calls the routine for that event type.
• The routine uses the current state and inputs to update the state , update
the FEL , and record customer outcomes .

Event routine example: arrival of customer i
• Suppose that the earliest entry in the FEL is ( t, A i ) , i.e., arrival of customer i
at time t .
• The simulation advances its clock to t , removes ( t, A i ) from the FEL, and
invokes the following arrival routine :
1. Record customer i ’s arrival time a i , and its spplied service duration s i .
2. If server idle: set B = 1 and assign customer i to the server. Record service
start b i = t and wait w i = 0 . Insert ( t + s i , D i ) to the FEL.
3. Otherwise: append ( i, a i , s i ) to the end of the queue status vector.
4. If another arrival exists, insert ( a i +1 , A i +1 ) to the FEL.

PAGE 17
2 KEY COMPONENTS OF DISCRETE-EVENT SIMULATION 17
Event routine example: service completion of customer i
• Suppose that the earliest entry in the FEL is ( t, D i ) , i.e., service completion of
customer i at time t .
• The simulation advances its clock to t , removes ( t, D i ) from the FEL, and
invokes the following completion routine :
1. Record customer i ’s departure time d i = t , and time-in-system v i = d i − a i .
2. If the queue is nonempty: remove its first customer, say j , and start their
service immediately. Record b j = t and wait w j = t − a j ; keep B = 1 and
insert ( t + s j , D j ) to the FEL.
3. Otherwise: set B = 0 .
A completion creates a new completion event only if another service starts . All
actions above occur at the same simulated time t .

Clock: simulated time vs. computer running time
Important: distinguish simulated time from computer running time .
• Simulated time measures how much time has elapsed in the system being
simulated.
• Computer running time measures how long the computer actually takes to
perform the simulation.
• In one second of computer running time, the simulation may advance by days,
years, or even centuries.
• This ability to compress time is one reason simulation is so useful: we can
study long-term system behavior without waiting for the same amount of real
time to pass.
A playful analogy: “ 天上一年，地上百年 ”—one year in one world may correspond
to a hundred years in another.
If our world were itself a simulation created by higher-dimensional beings, perhaps
our “years” of simulated time could pass within seconds of their computer running
time!

• A specified number of events: stop after a given number of events, e.g.,

• A fixed horizon: stop at a given simulation horizon T . For a fixed horizon, the
simulation processes events strictly before T and includes the last interval up
to T in the statistics.

DES overall logic: piecing together all components
Initialize once
• Set the clock t = 0 , initial state , and statistics ; specify the ending condition .
• Set the input source (supplied values or random-number generators ); put the
initial events into the FEL .
Repeat the event loop
1. If the ending condition is met or the FEL is empty, leave the loop.
2. Select the earliest event e in the FEL, with event time t e . For a fixed horizon
T , if t e ≥ T , leave the loop without processing e .
3. Accumulate time-based statistics over [ t, t e ) using the old state ; then advance
the clock to t e .
4. Remove e from the FEL and call its event routine : update state and statistics
records, obtain inputs as needed, and update the FEL. Return to step 1.
Finish and report
Compute performance measures from the statistics and report the results.

How to draw inputs?
Motivating question: Given a probability distribution with cumulative distribution
function (CDF) F , how can we generate X ∼ F ( ·) , so that P ( X ≤ x ) = F ( x ) ?
1. The building block: a uniform random variable U ∼ Uniform[0 , 1] . Given U ,
generate X = F
− 1
( U ) . It can be shown that X ∼ F ( ·) . This is called the
inverse-transform sampling.
2. How can a computer generate U ∼ Uniform[0 , 1] ? A fixed algorithm follows
a deterministic rule. Once its initial state is fixed, every output is determined,
so it is impossible to generate numbers that are truly random .
3. A pseudorandom-number generator (PRNG) uses a rule to update its
internal state and output numbers designed to behave like independent
uniform draws. A random seed sets its initial state, which leads to a
reproducible sequence of uniform draws.

LCG: the Hull–Dobell theorem
How to choose ( m, a, c ) ? Hull–Dobell theorem: For integers m ≥ 2 and
0 ≤ a, c < m , the LCG Z k +1 = ( aZ k + c ) mod m has full period m for every seed
Z 0 ∈ { 0 , . . . , m − 1 } if and only if :
1. c and m are relatively prime: gcd( c, m ) = 1 (greatest common divisor).
2. Every prime factor of m divides a − 1 .
3. If 4 divides m , then 4 also divides a − 1 .
Our example: m = 16 , a = 5 , c = 3 . Here gcd(3 , 16) = 1 , and both 2 (the only
prime factor of 16) and 4 divide a − 1 = 4 . Hence the period is exactly 16 .
Consequence of full period m : Every grid value 0 , 1 /m, . . . , ( m − 1) /m appears
once per full cycle, approximating a uniform distribution. In Nmypy , m = 2
128
However, full period does not imply truly randomness. The sequence of U 1 , U 2 , ...
generated from the LCG is deterministic and not independent by construction .

Week 3 summary
• Simulation automates bookkeeping. One run traces a sample path from
given inputs. Independent runs estimate average performance and variability.
• DES advances from event to event. The future-event list identifies what
happens next, and the clock jumps directly to that event’s time.
• State describes the system now, while statistics track performance.
Customer records give customer averages. Queue area and busy time divided
by T give average queue length and utilization.
• Hand simulation follows the same event loop. Initialize, select the earliest
event, accumulate statistics using the old state, advance the clock, and
execute its routine. Stop under the specified ending condition.
• Random inputs can be generated on the fly. Event routines draw interarrival
times and service durations from specified distributions, saving memory for
future inputs.
• Uniform draws are the building block for random inputs. Seeded PRNGs
such as LCGs give reproducible sequences. Inverse-transform and
acceptance–rejection sampling generate inputs with the required distribution.
