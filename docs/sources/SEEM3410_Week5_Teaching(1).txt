1 Data Scoping, Cleaning, and
Exploration
Before estimating distributions, investigate what the data can tell us.
1. Scope the available data. Identify which customers, operating periods,
and operating processes are recorded. This determines which input
distributions we can estimate.
2. Clean and document. Check missing, invalid, zero, and rounded
values. State filtering rules and identify censored measurements.
3. Explore through descriptive analysis. Compare customer/server
groups and clock-time periods or days. Look for differences suggesting
multiclass or time-varying arrivls.

2 Moment Matching (MOM)
We begin with parametric estimation : after choosing a family,
how can we use historical data to determine its parameters ?
Moment matching equates a chosen distribution’s theoretical moments
with the empirical moments of the historical data.
1. Choose a distribution family. Its support (e.g., nonnegative) and
shape (e.g., right-tailed) should be plausible for the input variable.
2. Choose and compute empirical moments. In standard moment
matching, use p moments for p unknown parameters , typically
starting with the lowest orders: the mean, second moment, etc.
3. Match theoretical and empirical moments. Solve the p
moment-matching equations for the p unknown parameters.

5 Accessing goodness of fit
• We have discussed several parametric and nonparametric estimation
methods . To quantify how well the estimated distributions fit the historical
data, we use goodness-of-fit tests .
• A discrepancy between the estimated distribution and the historical data is
inevitable because there is sampling variation on the finite historical data.
• Goodness-of-fit tests ask: Is the observed discrepancy larger than data
sampling variation would explain? If “yes”, then the estimated distribution is
not a good fit.
Three commonly used goodness-of-fit tests:
1. Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) test: find the largest discrepancy between the
empirical and estimated CDFs.
2. Anderson–Darling (A–D) test: accumulate CDF discrepancies , giving more
weight to the tails.
3. Chi-square test: compare discrepancies in historical and expected counts
over given intervals.

Why might a distribution fit poorly?
Possible reasons for the poor fits include:
• An unsuitable distribution family. The chosen family may miss the shape or
upper tail of service durations.
• Time-varying service patterns. Service durations may differ across times of
day, days, or shifts.
• Different server–customer-type pairs. Service durations may differ across
combinations of server type and customer type.
Data pooling can hide this heterogeneity: the pooled distribution is a mixture of
the segment distributions , which need not belong to a single distribution family.
A possible remedy: unpool the historical data.
• Segment the data by time period, server type, and customer type (or their
combinations).
• Estimate and assess a separate distribution within each segment.
• Keep enough records in each segment for reliable estimation.

7 Patience Estimation and Censoring
• So far, we have learned how to fit distributions based on uncensored data .
• The estimation of patience distributions are typically based on censored data.
• Let P i be denote the patience time of customer i , and C i their wait until
service. Instead of directly observing P i , we only observe
Y i = min( P i , C i ) , δ i = 1 { P i < C i } .
Outcome Record What it tells us about patience
Abandons ( Y i , δ i ) = ( y, 1) Patience ends at y : observe P i = y .
Enters service ( Y i , δ i ) = ( y, 0) Patience has not ended: P i > y .
• The served customer’s patience time is right-censored by their wait.
• Fitting the patience distribution using abandoned calls alone ( δ i = 1 )
describes P | P < C , only a selected subset.
• For calls entering services ( δ i = 0 ), treating their waiting times as patience
times is also incorrect .

• Start with available historical data. Scope, clean, and explore the records to
identify estimable inputs and differences across groups or periods.
• Fit a parametric distribution. MOM matches moments; MLE maximizes

• Use a nonparametric alternative. Resampling the empirical distribution

• Assess goodness of fit. K–S checks the largest CDF gap; A–D emphasizes
tails; Chi-square checks bin counts.
• Allow time-varying arrivals. Estimate each hourly rate as count/exposure;
check within-hour homogeneity and generate arrivals using the fitted rates.
• Account for censored patience. modify MLE to include survival probability in
