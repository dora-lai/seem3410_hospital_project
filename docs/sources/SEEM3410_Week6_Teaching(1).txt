1 Designing Simulation Experiments
Design of experiments (DOE) specifies which decision settings to simulate
and how to organize their runs to answer a system-improvement question.
1. Define the improvement question and performance measure .
2. Choose decision settings : specify each decision lever and its level.
3. Specify how to run the simulations and generate random inputs for
each decision setting.
4. Estimate how each decision lever affects performance, how levers
interact, and the uncertainty in these estimates .
In previous lectures, we have learned how to estimate performance at one
decision setting .
In this week, we will learn how to estimate the performance difference
between two settings, and combine such comparisons to identify individual
lever effects and interactions.

Running example: A service center with one FCFS waiting line
• In each day, the system starts empty and operates for eight hours.
Throughout a day, arrivals follow a Poisson process at λ = 1 . 5 /minute.
Baseline service duration is S i ∼ Gamma(2 , 0 . 5) .
• Decision lever 1: There are c identical servers; c = 2 is the baseline.
• Decision lever 2: Let v be the service speed multiplier v so that
customer i ’s service duration is S i /v :
– v = 1 is the baseline; v = 1 . 2 means 20% greater speed.
– Increasing v can represent investment in more training programs
or better service flow .
• Improvement question: How does adding a server (increase c ),
increasing service speed ( v ), or having both, reduce waiting time?
• Performance measure: Expected average customer waiting time .

Motivating question: How do we estimate the performance difference
between a proposal decision x 1 and a baseline x 0 ?
• For a given performance measure θ , our target is
∆ = θ ( x 1 ) − θ ( x 0 ) .
For waiting time, ∆ < 0 means the proposal improves performance.
• The point estimate is easy: subtract the two simulated means.
ˆ∆ = Y R ( x 1 ) − Y R ( x 0 ) .
How do we quantify the uncertainty in ˆ∆ ?
This depends on how the two sets of simulation outputs are generated.

• CRN uses a separate random stream for each stochastic mechanism

• Use a new set of random seeds for Run r + 1 , so D 1 , . . . , D R are

3 Full Factorial Designs
• §2 showed how to do paired comparisons for two decision settings.
• Comparing ( c, v ) = (2 , 1 . 0) with ( c, v ) = (3 , 1 . 2) changes both staﬀing
and speed levers, so the difference gives their combined effect .
• In DOE language, a lever is an experiment factor that can be varied.
To understand the contribution of each factor, we need to compare more
decision settings:
• Staﬀing effect: compare 2 and 3 servers at each speed level.
• Speed effect: compare speeds 1.0 and 1.2 at each staﬀing level.
• Interaction: does the effect of one lever depend on the other lever?
A factorial design specifies combinations of factor levels to compare, which
extends paired comparisons to estimate factor effects and interactions.

4 Response Surfaces and Metamodels
• Write each design point as x , e.g., x = ( c, v ) in our service-center
example, or x = ( c, v, λ ) if the demand scenario is also included.
• From a factorial design with D design points x 1 , . . . , x D , we obtain
estimates ¯Y R ( x i ) of the performance-response surface
θ ( c, v ) = E [ Y r ( c, v )]
at finitely many points x i , i = 1 , . . . , D .
• How can we predict performance θ ( c, v ) at an untested setting x ̸= x i ?
• A metamodel g ( c, v ) is a simple function fitted to these simulation
means to approximate the surface:
g ( c, v ) ≈ θ ( c, v ) .
• It predicts performance at untested settings ; these predictions need
to be checked by additional simulations.

Why build a metamodel after running the simulations?
Simulation gives noisy observations at finitely many settings . A fitted
metamodel extends those observations into a relationship we can use:
• Predict untested settings. Estimate performance between observed
points without running a new simulation for every trial setting.
• Explain tradeoffs. Study how predicted performance changes when one
or several decision levers change.
• Performance optimization through mathematical programming. The
metamodel g ( x ; ˆα ) can appear in the objective function or constraints.
Caveat: A promising decision predicted by the metamodel still requires
confirmation by simulation .

If validation fails, revise the experiment or metamodel
Choose the remedy according to the source of disagreement:
1. Imprecise simulation means. If simulation means have wide
confidence intervals , increase independent replications . More precise
simulation means can also improve the fitted coeﬀicients of
metamodels.
2. An inadequate metamodel form. If discrepancies persist with precise
simulation means, add design points where predictions are poor or
choose a more flexible form . For example, allow different speed curves
at different staﬀing levels, then refit.

Week 6 summary
• Plan the experiment before simulation. Specify the improvement question,
performance measure, decision levers with their levels, and run protocol.
• CRN can improve comparison precision. Use matched random inputs across
decision settings to reduce the impact of simulation noises.
• Factorial designs separate effects and interactions. Main effects average over
other levels; two- and three-factor interactions describe how conditional
effects change.
• Fit a metamodel by least squares. Choose a parametric form to approximate
the response surface, and fit the parameters by least squares.
• Validate metamodel predictions at untested settings. More replications
reduce sampling error; persistent discrepancies call for additional design points
or a better metamodel.
• Use the metamodel for decision optimization. Express costs, performance
targets, and feasibility using metamodels to formulate decision optimization
problems.
• Confirm the candidate decision independently. For the candidate decision,
confirm performance and improvement over baseline using fresh simulations.
