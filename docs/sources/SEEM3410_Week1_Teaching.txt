Why we cannot test every decision in the real system?
• A real intervention may be expensive, disruptive, slow, or unsafe.
• Hard to hold operating conditions ﬁxed for a fair comparison.
• Rare but important scenarios may not occur during a pilot.
• Some alternatives do not yet exist physically.
A model creates a controlled environment for asking:
“If we change the decision, what performance would we expect?”

What do we mean by a “model”?
A model is a purposeful abstraction of a real system: it retains the features
needed for an intended use and omits the rest. It is not a complete copy of reality.
A model makes explicit:
• intended use — the decision and performance measures that the stakeholder
cares about;
• boundary — system scope (what is inside and outside) and time horizon
(when the model begins and ends);
• level of detail — which components, states, and events are represented
individually or in aggregate;
• mechanisms — relationships, rules, and causal processes governing
interactions and state changes;
• inputs and assumptions — the decision setting being evaluated, assumptions
about how uncertainty is represented, and other simplifying assumptions.

How do we design a ﬁt-for-purpose model?
1. State the stakeholder’s decision and the alternatives to compare.
2. Select the performance measures that matter.
3. Draw a suitable system boundary that preserves the relevant
eﬀects.
4. Include mechanisms that are relevant to the comparison; abstract
the rest.
5. Clarify assumptions and limitations.
Start from the purpose , rather than the desire to make the
model detailed.

The focus of this course: service systems
Examples include a breakfast shop, an emergency department, a call center,
an airport checkpoint, and an urban mobility service.
Despite their diﬀerent contexts, they share common operational structure :
• customers (patients, passengers, calls, orders, or jobs) arrive over time;
• servers provide service with limited capacity (can be non-human);
• a queue forms when arriving customers must wait to be served;
• the service process involves customers moving through one or more
service stages under routing and priority rules;
• uncertainty in customer arrivals, service times, and behavior;
• performance is assessed through waiting time, queue length,
throughput, server utilization, service levels, and costs.

2 Analytical Evaluation for Canonical
Queueing Models
Queueing models capture the process ﬂow of many service systems and the
congestion created when uncertain demand meets limited capacity .
Arrivals − → Queue − → Service − → Departure / next stage
A queueing model speciﬁes the elements governing this typical ﬂow:
• arrival process : who arrives and what interarrival time distributions;
• service process : the number of servers and service time distributions;
• service discipline : service order within a queue (e.g., ﬁrst come ﬁrst
serve), routing (send an arriving job to which server?), and scheduling
(serve which job when a server gets idle?).
Some canonical queueing models can be evaluated analytically .
Kendall notation summarizes their main model assumptions.

Read the Kendall’s notation: A/S/c/K/ Disc + P
—the short form A/S/c assumes inﬁnite capacity ( K = ∞ ), FCFS
service discipline, and no abandonment.
• M/M/ 1 : Poisson arrivals, exponential service times, one server.
• M/M/c : Poisson arrivals, exponential service times, c servers.
• M/M/c/c : c servers and no waiting room; arrivals that ﬁnd no
server idle are lost.
• M/M/ 1 + M : one server and exponential patience; customers
may abandon.
• G/G/ 1 : general interarrival and service distributions, one server.
Usually, the assumption of Poisson arrivals and exponential service
or patience times makes a model analytically tractable, due to its
memoryless (Markovian) property.

Little’s law: a universal analytical relationship
L = λW
• L : long-run average number of customers inside a deﬁned system.
• λ : long-run eﬀective rate at which customers enter that system.
• W : long-run average time an customer spends inside it.
The three quantities must use the same system boundary and
compatible time units .
• Knowing any two of the three allows the third to be determined.
• Very general and universal . It only requires stability of the
queueing model, and does not require exponential distributions.
• It is discovered by John Little, an American operations researcher
(1928 - 2024)

PAGE 21
2 ANALYTICAL EV ALUATION FOR CANONICAL QUEUEING MODELS 21
Little’s law applies at diﬀerent boundaries
Boundary Identity Interpretation
Whole system L = λW Num. of customers in system
Waiting line L q = λW q Num. of customers in queue
Servers L s = λ E [ S ] Num. customers in service
• E [ S ] is the mean service time of customer;
• W q is the mean time a customer spent waiting in queue;
• W = E [ S ] + W q is the mean time a customer spent in the system
(waiting plus service)
• Hence, L = L q + L s .

3 Simulation Evaluation for Complex
Models
Closed-form evaluation works for simple service models, but more complex
models may contain:
• multiple stages, shared resources, feedback, and complex routing;
• priorities, appointments, abandonment, and blocking;
• time-varying arrival, correlated service times, and non-exponential
empirical distributions;
• service policies whose eﬀects cannot be isolated algebraically.
Oftentime, complex models cannot be evaluated in closed-form,
and simulation-based evaluation becomes necessary.

PAGE 30
3 SIMULATION EV ALUATION FOR COMPLEX MODELS 30
Simulation evaluates a model through computational experimentation
One simulation run:
Simulation logic+ decision scenario + one realization of random inputs
− → one possible system history and performance outputs
• Specify a decision scenario and sample one realization of the stochastic
inputs (e.g., customer arrival times, service times, and patience times).
• Execute the simulation logic over a speciﬁed simulation time to
generate a system history and its outputs.
• Repeat simulation runs with new random-input realizations to estimate
means and variability of outputs
• Compare simulated outputs under diﬀerent decision scenarios to
quantify decision impact.
• Simulation requires repeated, statistically designed runs.

• Analytical evaluation is what we hope for: it is fast, transparent, and
insightful—but useful analytical results are often hard to obtain.
• Discover patterns: simulated outputs can suggest identities or monotonicity
that guides a mathematical proof for analytical evaluation.
• Check formulas: after deriving an analytical result, compare it with simulation;
disagreement reveals either a proof error or a simulation code error.
• Simulation evaluation is the safety net: use it when analytical results are
unavailable, and as a computational benchmark when they are available.

Week 1 summary
• Start with the decision. A model represents the parts of a real system needed
to evaluate performance and decision impact.
• Models are ﬁt for purpose. The decision need determines the model’s
boundaries, mechanisms, inputs and assumptions, and outputs; the same
system can therefore have diﬀerent models.
• Service systems share a common structure. Customers arrive, move through
service stages, wait for servers, and follow service rules.
• Queueing models are canonical service-system models. Kendall notation
describes their structure; key outputs include utilization, queue length, and
waiting time.
• Analytical evaluation is preferred when tractable. It is fast, transparent, and
insightful; M/M/ 1 formulas reveal how congestion grows as utilization
increases. Little’s law describes a universal relation between waiting time and
queue length.
• Simulation evaluation is the safety net for complex models. Repeated
computational experiments estimate performance, reveal patterns, and provide
benchmarks for checking formulas.
